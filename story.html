My Story

Why Saudi Arabia — In My Own Words

Realizing that life is limited forced me to confront the gap between the person I imagined becoming and the actions I had taken toward it.

At seventeen, I became aware of the distance between the life I had imagined and the life I was working toward. I had always believed that by that age I would have already accomplished something, moved forward toward my goals. Instead, I spent my seventeenth birthday confronting an uncomfortable truth: looking around, I realized no one had even remembered it. The following day, I learned that someone I had seen only a few months earlier had passed away at the age of twenty-two. Until then, death had always been a distant idea — something I knew in theory but had never experienced personally. The news didn't answer all my problems, but it changed how I thought about time and responsibility. I realized that building a meaningful life takes disciplined effort, one step at a time.

Determined, I began to change my approach to studying. The process wasn't immediate — early results were disappointing — but procrastinating and hesitating were no longer options. Learning gradually became enjoyable; things that once felt confusing started to make sense. As my confidence grew, so did my interest in subjects like Accountancy, Business Studies, and Economics. I learned them by seeing their real-world application — the systems behind how organizations make decisions, manage people, gather resources, and structure hierarchy; the roles, responsibilities, finances, day-to-day operations, and unwritten norms that let them function and predict each other's behavior. That was when I started shaping the academic direction I wanted to pursue.

My vice principal, Mrs. Hussina Kandewalla, noticed the change in me. She saw that I had become more attentive in class and had started helping my peers understand topics I used to struggle with myself, trying to help them find the same interest in learning. Her encouragement gave me the confidence to apply for a leadership position at my school. Soon after, I was appointed House Captain — she saw leadership qualities in me before I recognized them in myself.

Becoming House Captain taught me what leadership actually requires: understanding people, communicating clearly, working as a team, and staying responsible even when things get difficult. Organizing events with my fellow captains showed me that a group succeeds when everyone's goals are acknowledged and the team moves in a common direction.

A few months later, a teacher recommended me to volunteer at a Free Medical Camp for Bangladeshi expatriates in Kuwait. I helped prepare food to be handed out during the event, and assisted and supported patients who came seeking basic medical care and guidance. Seeing elderly people and workers managing health concerns alongside financial pressures gave me a deeper understanding of the challenges people face. That experience taught me that organized service takes empathy, planning, and cooperation.

Saudi Arabia is more than an innovation hub to me — it feels personal and meaningful. My connection to it is both religious and regional, shaped by having been born and raised in the Gulf. It offers the chance to learn within a large, dynamic market shaped by people of many nationalities and cultures, and I've come to love the Gulf's tradition of welcoming guests with generosity, respect, and dignity. My experience volunteering with the Bangladeshi community in Kuwait strengthened this understanding — showing me that progress means the most when it comes with empathy, responsibility, and service.

Studying in Saudi Arabia would let me understand how businesses in this region are managed, how they adapt to change, and how they create sustainable value. I want to learn how organizations combine innovation, effective management, and social responsibility to create lasting value for their customers and communities. I believe this environment would help me grow — and grow into someone who can contribute to the Gulf region's future.
